Remember you telling a story. Should have a beginning, a middle, and an end. 
Respect the time allotted for the sake of your audience. 
If you go over time, they are no longer focusing on the talk, but watching the clock to see when you are going to stop. 
Explain at the level of the class. 
Have a technical term, define it. If you don't quite understand it, no one will. 
Because we are all novices, you will need to have a long intro. 
But try to engage the audience. 
Do not read from your slides; use notecards, if you must.

Plasmids are small, circular DNA molecules independent of the chromosomal DNA that can replicate autonomously within a bacterial cell. They are often used as vectors in molecular biology to carry foreign DNA into a host organism.
There are two main types of plasmid vectors:
Low-Copy Number Plasmids
Copy number: 1-5 copies per cell
Applications:
Stable and controlled gene expression
Expression of toxic proteins
Challenges:
Low yield of plasmid DNA
Instability and potential loss of the plasmid
High-Copy Number Plasmids
Copy number: 50-300+ copies per cell
Applications:
High yield of proteins and recombinant products
Large-scale plasmid DNA preparation
Challenges:
Overexpression of the inserted gene can be toxic to the host cell
Increased risk of plasmid loss due to the burden on the cell
An example of a high-copy number plasmid is pUC19. Derived from the pMB1 replicon, it's widely used for cloning small DNA fragments. Key features include:
A multiple cloning site (MCS) for inserting foreign DNA
A lacZ gene for blue/white screening of recombinant clones
High copy number for efficient plasmid DNA production
Compatibility with various restriction enzymes for versatile cloning experiments
In conclusion, plasmid vectors are essential tools in molecular biology for manipulating and studying DNA. The choice between low-copy and high-copy number plasmids depends on the specific application and the desired outcome

11 Patents in BioTechnology- Shubham
 
How do patent practices distinguish between naturally occurring entities and synthetic creations in biotech? 
Naturally Occurring Entities: Patent practices generally do not allow the patenting of naturally occurring entities, such as naturally-occurring DNA sequences, proteins, or naturally-occurring organisms. Isolation, purification, or discovery of such entities is typically not considered inventive enough to merit a patent.
 Synthetic Creations: Synthetic creations, such as genetically modified organisms (GMOs) or artificial life forms created through genetic engineering or synthetic biology techniques, may be patentable. The key here is that the creation represents a human-made, non-natural invention that is novel, non-obvious, and useful.
Ownership and Ethical Considerations:The entity or individual that holds the patent has exclusive rights to make, use, sell, and license the patented life form for a specified period (usually 20 years from the date of filing).

SET POINTS 6 - JANNELAAn NDA includes a set duration, specifying the time for confidentiality obligations, and outlines each party's responsibilities to protect the information. Certain NDAs may have a perpetual confidentiality obligation, meaning the commitment to keep information confidential lasts indefinitely and can be subject to negotiation. An attorney's fee provision designates the party responsible for legal fees in case of a breach, impacting the financial consequences of any dispute. NDAs in biotech partnerships protect information but may limit collaboration. The ability to balance confidentiality and collaboration is pivotal for success. While Biotech NDAs protect proprietary information, they may hinder the free exchange of data and collaboration among researchers. Clear terms in NDAs that allow disclosure for academic and peer review purposes can help mitigate potential conflicts between confidentiality requirements and the openness necessary for scientific progress.

#1 Defective Genes - Sonia
Polymerase Chain Reaction
Hereditary - involve more than one gene ex: down syndrome bc extra copy of chrom 21
Haplo-insufficiency (loss of function) - one functional copy of gene is insufficient ex: ELN gene maskes elastin. If one copy - narrow aorta and have a congenital aortic stenosis
Dominant mutations (change or gain of function) - single mutant allele cause significant phenotypic effects. Ex achondroplasia or dwarfism.
Tandem Repeats/Dynamic Mutations - reapets of codons are normal (5-30), but beyond threshold can cause disease. Number can expand and vary, hence dynamic mutation ex: Fragile X Syndrome (CGG) carriers up to 200 repeats and recessive up to 1200 repeats.
Imprinting -  Due to one copy of gene being methylated and inhibits its gene expression. Ex: Prader Willi Syndrome or Angelman Syndrome
Mitochondrial Defects - mtDNA from mom) VS nuclearDNA from dad. Ex: NARP syndrome - Neuropathy, ataxia, retinitis pigmentosa. MtDNA gene mutation -> ATP production slowed -> muscle weakness, seizures, sensory malfunction.

Candidate cloning - analyze sx and make informed guess depending on what kinds of proteins involved. Rarely successful, but proteomics research and help from AI is becoming more realistic.
Knockout Mice - using model organism mice and artificially inactivate genes (knock out) and than analyze symptoms.
Functional Cloning - Take a suspected protein involved in disorder, then amino acid seq determined, then deduce coding sequence of gene.
Positional Cloning - When gene product is unknown. Involved gene mapping and linkage studies. Markers used like SNPs, RFLPs, & VNTRs.

PCR = Can amplify genes or regions of genes, to identify gene defects. 1) DNA Extraction 2) Amplification via thermal cycling 3) Analysis via gel electrophoresis or sequencing machines

Are there any gene therapy approaches for cancer? How does gene therapy for cancer differ from that for inherited diseases like CF?
Suicide gene therapy selectively targets and destroys cancer cells by introducing a therapeutic gene that converts a prodrug into a toxic compound.  It is designed to enhance the precision and specificity of cancer treatment by targeting only the cancer cells that have been genetically modified to express the therapeutic enzyme. Unlike suicide gene therapy, Gene therapy for inherited diseases like cystic fibrosis aims to correct or replace the mutated genes responsible for the condition. The goal is to restore the function of the affected gene or provide a functional copy to address the genetic defect.

How do oncolytic viruses function in gene therapy? What are the biomarkers used to identify cancerous cells vs. normal cells?
Oncolytic viruses are specialized gene therapy that infect and replicate within cancer cells while sparing healthy cells. They can also carry genes that induce cancer cell death, enhancing their cancer-targeting ability. Cancer cell biomarkers, such as proteins and genetic mutations like EGFR differentiate cancer cells from normal ones.

What trials or treatments have shown promising results? Are there any FDA-approved gene therapy treatments for cancer?
CRISPR is a bacterial immune system that remembers past virus infections and protects against reinfection by integrating viral DNA into the bacterial genome. It's been modified into a gene-editing tool, with CRISPR/Cas9 being the most common version. Herceptin is a targeted therapy for HER2-positive breast cancer, blocking the HER2 protein to inhibit cancer cell growth. It's not a gene therapy and is often used alongside other treatments.

Research in this area is ongoing, and various nanoparticle formulations are being tested in preclinical and clinical trials for cancer gene therapy. These nanoparticles have the potential to improve the precision, efficacy, and safety of gene therapy in cancer treatment. However, it's important to note that the field of nanoparticle-based gene therapy is still evolving, and clinical translation requires thorough testing and evaluation for safety and efficacy.

How does the Ames test determine if chemicals are mutagenic? What are the limitations of Ames tests in predicting mutagenicity? What are the molecular mechanisms for a substance to induce mutations?
The Ames test is typically performed using bacteria, often strains of Salmonella typhimurium, which have mutations that make them unable to synthesize specific amino acids. These mutant bacteria require these amino acids to grow. the mutagenicity of the substance is determined by comparing the number of colonies in the treated and untreated samples. limitations  1.The Ames test uses bacteria, which are different from human cells.so it is Relevance to humans,2.False negatives and positives 3.Many mutagenic compounds require metabolic activation in the body to become mutagenic. The Ames test may not reflect these activation processes accurately 4.Limited endpoints. molecular mechanisms Base substitution,Insertions and deletions,Chemical modification and Cross-linking
Ethidium Bromide is a strong mutagen in the Ames test, but showed little to no carcinogenic effects in mice. Why? Are there any common substances previously thought to be safe but are now considered mutagenic?
Ethidium bromide is a chemical compound commonly used in molecular biology and genetics to stain DNA and RNA. lack of carcinogenicity can be explained by several factors 1.Differences in species sensitivity 2.Metabolic activation 3.Exposure levels 4.Genetic factors common substances  1.Radon gas: Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that can accumulate in homes. It was not widely recognized as a health hazard until studies linked it to lung cancer. 2.Benzene: Benzene, a solvent used in various industries, was initially considered safe. However, it is now known to be a human carcinogen.
How do substances promote cancer without being mutagenic? Explain and provide an example of how a substance can be a carcinogen and not a mutagen.
Radon is a radioactive gas that can be found in the soil and rocks. It emits alpha particles, which are highly ionizing radiation. When radon gas is inhaled, it can decay within the lungs, releasing these alpha particles. These alpha particles can damage the surrounding lung tissue, causing inflammation and oxidative stress. This damage and inflammation can lead to the promotion of cancer, particularly lung cancer, by creating an environment in which cell growth and division are disrupted.

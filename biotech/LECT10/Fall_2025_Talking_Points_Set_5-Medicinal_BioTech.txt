#6 Retroviruses and Lentiviruses in Gene Therapy
Retroviruses in Gene Therapy
Integrating Their Genetic Information
Retroviruses are used to deliver therapeutic genes by integrating them into the host genome. They reverse-transcribe their RNA genome into DNA, which is then inserted into the host's chromosomes.
Insertional mutagenesis can activate oncogenes or disrupt tumor suppressors, causing cancer. Early SCID trials showed leukemia risk, leading to the design of self-inactivating vectors and safer promoter choices.
A suicide gene allows selective elimination of modified cells if adverse effects occur. HSCs and T cells are commonly modified ex vivo, allowing controlled expansion and testing before patient infusion.
HIV was engineered as a lentiviral vector to deliver anti-leukemia genes into hematopoietic cells. Retroviruses have smaller payloads than adenoviruses but integrate into dividing cells for durable expression.
Yes, products like Strimvelis and certain CAR-T therapies use retroviral vectors for gene delivery.

 How does the Ames test determine if chemicals are mutagenic? What are the limitations of Ames tests in predicting mutagenicity? What are the molecular mechanisms for a substance to induce mutations?
The Ames test is a biological assay that uses mutant strains of Salmonella typhimurium that cannot synthesize histidine. The suspected mutagen is then mixed with these bacterial strains and plated. If the substance causes mutations, reversing the initial mutation, the bacteria would restore histidine production and thus colonies will grow. The amount of colonies determines mutagenic potential and potency of the tested compound.
It is used on a bacterial system which differs from human system.  To mimic mammalian metabolism, rat liver S9 fraction is used in conjunction with the Ames test. However, there are still differences in metabolic pathways between rats and humans that can affect a compound's mutagenic classification.

#8 Dutch elm disease
Gary Strobel
Dutch elm disease is caused by a fungal pathogen that spreads mainly through elm bark beetles, including Scolytus multistriatus and Hylurgopinus rufipes. The fungus blocks the tree&#8217;s water flow, causing wilting and death. Gary Strobel once claimed he found a cure by using a weakened fungal strain, but his results were never reproducible and his work was criticized for poor controls and scientific misconduct. Today, Dutch elm disease is managed with trunk injections of fungicides, sanitation and removal of infected trees, planting resistant elm varieties, and controlling bark beetle populations.&#8221;
&#8220;Dutch elm disease can be compared to the emerald ash borer, Agrilus planipennis, an invasive beetle whose larvae tunnel under the bark and kill ash trees quickly. To fight the ash borer, scientists use quarantines on moving firewood, biological control using parasitic wasps, removal of infested trees, resistant ash breeding, and purple sticky traps or trap trees to monitor and reduce the beetle population.&#8221;
&#8220;Both threats caused major ecological and economic damage by removing major shade trees from forests and cities. Dutch elm disease strategies focus on stopping a fungus and its beetle vectors, while ash borer strategies target the insect itself.&#8221;
&#8220;Finally, using genetic engineering to save tree species raises ethical concerns, including ecological risks, long-term effects on wild forests, genetic contamination of natural tree populations, corporate ownership of engineered trees, and public trust. These issues need to be carefully balanced when considering genetic solutions to tree diseases.

Hello everyone!
Degenerate primers may seem confusing at first but let’s try to understand together.Normally, you need to know a DNA sequence to make a perfect primer. But, what if you don’t? Degenerate primers are the answer. If we know the amino acid sequence of a protein, we can create a set of slightly different primers that cover all of the possible DNA codes for those amino acids. This mixture ensures at least one primer will be a perfect fit, allowing us to find and amplify the gene.

Think of PCR as a molecular copy machine that runs in a simple, repeating loop. First, you “unzip” the original document with high heat. (201-208f) Then, you place the “start and end” markers (primers) on the document by cooling it down. (122-149f) Finally, you let the “copying arm” (polymerase) run along, creating a new copy. (162f) Each loop makes twice as many copies as you started with. If you do this 20 times you already have over a million copies.
The reaction is heated to a high temperature to break the hydrogen bonds between the two strands of the DNA double helix. This separates the DNA into single strands, making them accessible to the primers and polymerase. 
The temperature is lowered to allow the primers to bind (anneal) to their complementary sequences on the single-stranded DNA template.
The temperature is raised to the optimal temperature for the DNA polymerase to synthesize new DNA strands, starting from the primers.

The biggest breakthrough was finding a heat-stable enzyme called Taq polymerase. Before, the enzyme used for copying DNA would be destroyed by the high heat. But Taq could survive it, which meant scientists no longer had to add new enzyme for every single cycle. This made PCR automatic, fast, and much more practical.

Challenges in Scaling Up DNA Circuits
Scaling DNA circuits for broader applications faces several hurdles:
Signal Leakage: Unintended binding events can generate false signals. As circuit complexity increases, leakage accumulates.
Kinetic Limitations: DNA reactions are slower than electronic circuits, limiting speed and real-time response.
Resource Competition: Multiple DNA strands in a cell compete for limited cellular machinery or molecular resources, reducing efficiency.
Design Complexity: Creating reliable multi-layered logic circuits is difficult due to cross-reactivity between DNA strands.
Stability: DNA circuits are prone to degradation by nucleases in biological environments.

# Pedagogical Lecture Slide Style Guide

## 60-Second Summary

Make students **think, look, and listen**. If the slide asks students to read too much, decode tiny text, or hunt for the main idea, the slide is getting in the way.

Practical defaults:

- start every deck with learning objectives (each deck, not just the first deck of a lecture)
- end every deck with summary slides (each deck, not just the last deck of a lecture)
- use images, diagrams, graphs, structures, or comparisons as the main teaching tool
- keep slide text short enough to read at a glance
- put the spoken explanation in speaker notes, not on the slide
- use one main idea per slide
- use section dividers to reset the room
- use flip books and alternate views when a concept benefits from stepwise reveal
- ask before telling whenever a concept benefits from prediction or recall
- build in think-pair-share, prediction, graphical interpretation, or misconception checkpoints
- use consistent layouts across a lecture series

## Scope and Audience

This guide is primarily for building lecture slides in a visual, instructor-led teaching style. It can also be shared with TAs, co-instructors, and collaborators so that slides across a course feel consistent.

The examples are often biochemistry-flavored because that is the main teaching context. Mechanisms, structures, pathways, and graphs should be treated as examples, not limits. The same principles apply to other scientific courses.

When used in a project, the canonical file should be named `style-guide.md`. Older versioned files are archive copies. New slide work should treat `style-guide.md` as the source of truth unless the instructor says otherwise.

## AI-Assisted Authoring Rules

When using AI to help build slides, the AI should follow this guide as the default style.

AI should:

- preserve the instructor's teaching voice
- ask before adding content not found in the source material
- never invent figures, citations, statistics, quotes, or source claims
- default to placeholders when a figure, structure, or image is needed
- if asked to recreate a figure, leave a labeled placeholder rather than drawing an unsupported figure
- keep slide text minimal
- put full explanations in notes
- suggest active-learning prompts when the material invites student interpretation
- avoid inventing examples, sources, or claims without checking

If source material is incomplete, the slide should show a placeholder or note rather than adding unsupported content.

## Core Philosophy

Be a visual storyteller. The slide should support the story, not replace the instructor. Students should be able to glance at the slide, understand the main idea, and then listen to the explanation.

The instructor does the teaching. The slide provides the visual anchor.

It should not make students read paragraphs during class.

## Slide Design Principles

### 1. Use images as the main teaching tool

Most slides should include images, diagrams, structures, examples, graphs, or visual comparisons.

Use visuals to:

- introduce a concept
- show a process
- compare two ideas
- interpret evidence
- connect the topic to everyday experience
- reduce written explanation

Images are expected, not optional. A slide with only text should be rare and should have a clear purpose.

### 2. Keep text short

Slides should not contain complete sentences unless absolutely necessary.

Use:

- short phrases
- keywords
- labels
- prompts
- simple definitions
- brief comparisons

Avoid paragraphs. Avoid long bullet points. Avoid putting the full spoken explanation on the slide. If the slide reads like a handout, it has too much prose. Split the idea or move the prose to notes.

### 3. Put the explanation in the speaker notes

The slide should not contain everything the instructor plans to say.

Use speaker notes for:

- full explanations
- transitions
- examples
- jokes or personal comments
- reminders about emphasis
- longer definitions
- expected answers to questions
- timing for active-learning beats

Students should look at the slide and listen to the instructor, not read silently while the instructor talks.

### 4. Teach one main idea per slide

Each slide should have a clear purpose. Before keeping a slide, ask:

- What should students learn here?
- What should students look at first?
- What will I say that is not already on the slide?

If a slide has too many ideas, split it into multiple slides.

## Pedagogical Structure

### Split lectures across multiple decks

A single lecture intentionally spans multiple slide decks. Presentation software (Keynote, PowerPoint) stores each deck as a zip archive, and every save rezips the entire archive. Large decks produce long save lags that interrupt authoring.

Keep individual decks small so saves stay fast. Do not merge decks back together for tidiness; the split is a practical file-format choice, not a pedagogical one.

### Start with learning objectives

Every slide deck should start with learning objectives, including each deck within a multi-deck lecture, not only the first deck. Learning objectives should be specific and student-centered.

Good objectives use action verbs such as:

- identify
- describe
- compare
- explain
- trace
- predict
- classify
- interpret

Avoid vague objectives such as "understand" unless the objective also states what students should be able to do.

### Add an orientation slide when the deck needs a map

After the title slide and objectives, use a short orientation slide when the lecture belongs to a larger unit or multi-lecture sequence. This can be a chapter map, unit outline, or "Part 1 / Part 2" divider.

Keep the orientation short. The goal is to show students where they are, not to preview every detail.

### Use the ask-before-telling rhythm

Before defining a term, solving a problem, interpreting a figure, or naming a mechanism, ask the question first. This gives students a reason to listen for the answer.

Good uses include:

- direct recall: "What are the three components?"
- prediction: "What happens next?"
- interpretation: "What does this graph show?"
- comparison: "Which one is different?"
- case opening: "How could DNA identify this person?"

Use a large, centered question slide. Put the answer on the next slide or reveal the answer after students think or discuss.

### Use case-story openings when motivation matters

A short case, puzzle, news story, clinical example, or real-world consequence can create motivation before technical content. Keep the story short and return quickly to the scientific tool or concept.

Good pattern:

1. brief case or puzzle
2. student prediction or question
3. scientific method or concept
4. return to the case

### Build from simple to complex

Begin with the familiar version of an idea, then add detail. For abstract concepts, use a familiar physical analogy before the technical version. The analogy should be visual, short, and easy to leave behind once the real concept is clear.

A useful concept-building sequence is:

1. everyday example
2. simple question
3. basic definition
4. visual model
5. molecular or mechanistic detail
6. application or comparison

Add section bookends separately: an active-learning beat when students should process the idea, and a takeaway slide when the section needs closure.

### Define technical terms briefly

Explain at the level of the class. If a technical term appears on a slide, define the term clearly.

Use this format on the slide:

**technical term** = **3 to 5 word definition**

Put the longer explanation in the notes. This resolves the tension between defining terms and keeping slides visual.

If the instructor cannot explain a term simply, students will not understand it either.

### Use question-answer pairs for core vocabulary

For vocabulary students must know, use a recall question slide before the answer slide. This is different from think-pair-share because the goal is quick retrieval, not extended discussion.

Good pattern:

1. "Name the two types of nucleobases"
2. pause
3. show the visual answer
4. connect the term to the next concept

### Use mnemonics only when they earn their space

Default to no mnemonic. Add one only when students must memorize categories, names, structures, or order, and the mnemonic is easier than the raw list. Use mnemonics when they are short, visual, and easy to repeat.

Avoid long mnemonics that require more work than the concept. Put the longer explanation in notes.

### Use comparisons to clarify concepts

Comparison slides are especially useful for science teaching.

Use comparisons to show:

- before vs. after
- active vs. inactive
- normal vs. altered
- structure vs. function
- similar terms with different meanings
- two mechanisms students may confuse

Visual comparisons are usually better than text-only comparisons.

When two terms are easily confused, make the confusion explicit. A contrast slide should answer: "How are these similar, and what is the one difference that matters?"

### Add misconception checkpoints

Use misconception checkpoints for ideas students often get wrong. These slides are teaching strategy, not just layout.

Good pattern:

1. ask the tempting wrong idea as a question
2. let students predict or discuss
3. show the corrected visual
4. name the takeaway in one short phrase

Keep the tone low-stakes. The goal is to make the misconception visible, not to embarrass students.


### Choose the right question-slide type

Several slide types use questions, but they do different jobs. Pick the format based on what the instructor wants students to do.

| Type | Main purpose | Best visual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Ask-before-telling | Create curiosity before a new idea | Large centered question, no answer |
| Vocabulary question-answer | Quick recall of a core term | Question slide, then visual answer slide |
| Multiple-choice question | Self-assessment or exam-style check | Short stem, few choices, answer revealed later |
| Think-pair-share | Reasoning and discussion | Prompt plus timing and partner instruction |
| Graphical interpretation | Build data and figure literacy | Large graph or figure plus an interpretation prompt |
| Misconception checkpoint | Surface and correct a tempting wrong idea | Question, pause, corrected visual, takeaway |

### Use section dividers and transitions

Use section divider slides to signal a major topic change. These slides help the instructor and students reset before the next idea. They should be visually distinct but simple. Keep them visually quiet so the room can reset.

Good divider slides include:

- a short section title
- one image or icon
- a question that previews the next section
- little or no explanatory text

Use transitions to help students see why the next topic follows from the previous one. Put the spoken transition in the notes.

### End with summary and takeaway slides

Every slide deck should end with a summary slide, including each deck within a multi-deck lecture, not only the last deck. End each major section with a short takeaway slide, and end the lecture with a final summary slide.

A takeaway slide should name the **3 to 5 ideas** students should remember.

Use short phrases, not full explanations. Put the review explanation in the notes.

### Keep visual grammar consistent across a series

For a lecture series, use repeated layouts for repeated jobs.

Keep these consistent across lectures:

- title slides
- learning objectives
- section dividers
- question slides
- active-learning beats
- comparison slides
- mechanism slides
- takeaway slides
- summary slides

Students should learn the visual grammar once and then use it to follow the lecture.

## Student Engagement

### Build in active-learning beats

Question slides are useful, but active-learning beats are more structured. They signal a mode change in the room. Use active-learning beats when students should do something, not just think quietly.

Good active-learning beats include:

- think-pair-share
- predict the next step
- choose between two options
- interpret a graph, figure, or structure
- label part of a diagram
- explain a term to a neighbor
- identify what changed
- find the mistake
- connect the idea to a familiar example

Use these often enough that students expect to participate. A good default is one active-learning beat every 10 to 15 minutes.

### Make multiple-choice questions serve a purpose

Multiple-choice slides are allowed when they help students self-assess. They are especially useful for checking vocabulary, distinguishing common misconceptions, or preparing students for exam-style reasoning.

However, multiple-choice slides should not be the main form of interaction in an in-person class. Without polling, they give the instructor less live feedback than they did in Zoom-based courses. When the goal is discussion or interpretation, adapt the MC question into think-pair-share, prediction, or graphical interpretation.

Use MC questions when self-assessment value is high. Use think-pair-share when the instructor wants reasoning, discussion, or feedback from the room.

### Format active-learning beats clearly

Active-learning beat slides should look different from normal content slides.

Recommended format:

- large prompt at the top or center
- clear action word: **Predict**, **Discuss**, **Compare**, **Label**, **Explain**, **Interpret**
- visible timing: **30 sec alone**, **1 min with partner**, **share out**
- one image, diagram, graph, or choice set
- no answer on the slide unless revealed later

Put the expected answer, timing, and follow-up explanation in the notes.

### Use think-pair-share

Use think-pair-share for concepts that are important, confusing, or worth discussion. Graphical interpretation is especially valuable because it builds graphical literacy and gives students a reason to talk through evidence.

Simple pattern:

1. Think: students answer alone
2. Pair: students compare with a neighbor
3. Share: a few students report out

Keep the slide prompt short. Put the timing, expected answer, and follow-up explanation in the notes. Useful prompts include: "What does this graph show?", "How would you interpret this information?", "What changed between these two views?", and "What evidence supports that conclusion?"

### Design slides that invite participation

Question slides, comparison slides, incomplete diagrams, graphs, and prediction prompts help students stay active. Avoid making every slide a finished explanation. Sometimes the best slide creates a small gap that students can fill during class.

## Pacing and Slide Count

Pacing is part of slide design. This style uses many minimal visual slides, so slide count alone is not a good measure of pace.

The instructor typically plans around **100 slides per hour**. That works out to an average of about **30 to 40 seconds per slide**. The median slide is faster, often **10 to 20 seconds**, because many slides are quick visual beats. Interpretation slides, active-learning beats, mechanisms, calculations, and summaries pull the average upward.

For a **90-minute lecture**, use roughly **150 slides** as a planning anchor. This is not a quota. It is a calibration point for this minimal, fast-moving slide style.

Use these planning defaults:

- 10 to 20 seconds for many minimal visual slides
- 30 to 40 seconds as the overall average pace
- about 100 slides per hour as a rough planning anchor
- 1 to 2 minutes for a short active-learning beat
- 3 to 5 minutes for a fuller think-pair-share
- more slides for flip books, reveals, and alternate views of the same content
- fewer slides when students must interpret a dense figure, graph, table, equation, or mechanism

Do not judge pacing only by slide count. A minimal deck may move quickly because each slide carries one small visual step. A single graph or mechanism may take several minutes if students interpret it together. A title or transition slide may take only 10 seconds.

Budget time before class:

- opening and objectives
- each major section
- active-learning beats
- transitions
- summary and wrap-up

If time is tight, remove detail slides before removing active-learning beats or takeaways.

## Accessibility and Font Rules

### Make text readable from the back of the room

Text that looks readable on a laptop may be too small in the classroom. If students struggle to read the slide, they will stop listening and start decoding the text.

Use large text for all slide content. Prioritize a few readable words over many small words. When a slide needs smaller text, move the detail to the notes or split the slide into several slides.

Use a quick test before class: step back from the screen or view the slide in sorter mode. If the main point is not readable at a glance, the slide has too much text or the font is too small.

### Preferred fonts

Use **OpenDyslexic** as the preferred slide font. It works well for short text on slides, especially titles, prompts, labels, and keywords.

Use **PT Sans Narrow** for long URLs, citation-like text, or compact technical strings that need to fit without becoming unreadable.

Keep font choices simple and consistent. Do not mix many fonts on one slide. Use font size, spacing, and placement to create hierarchy instead of adding extra styles.

Avoid shrinking text to make content fit. When text does not fit, reduce the words, move the explanation to notes, or split the idea across slides.

### Minimum readability standards

Use large, readable type. The exact size depends on the classroom, but the guiding rule is simple: students in the back should read the slide at a glance.

Practical defaults:

- large titles and prompts
- readable labels on diagrams
- no tiny captions that carry essential information
- no dense tables unless simplified or zoomed
- no screenshots with unreadable embedded text

### Contrast and color

Use strong contrast between text and background. Avoid low-contrast text, especially on gradient or photo backgrounds.

Use color as a reasoning cue, not decoration. Color should mark meaning, focus, category, contrast, or the current step in a reveal.

Examples:

- meaning: red = active site, gray = inactive partner
- focus: bright outline = current structure being discussed
- category: one color for substrate, another for product
- contrast: wild type vs. mutant
- current step: highlighted row or arrow in a flip-book sequence

Do not communicate information by color alone. Pair color with labels, shapes, arrows, position, or text.

### Visual accessibility

Avoid overcrowding. White space helps students find the important object quickly.

For complex figures, either simplify the figure or reveal the idea across several slides.

## Text Rules

### Use short slide text

Preferred:

- "hydrophobic core"
- "signal amplification"
- "light to neural signal"
- "structure determines function"
- "GPCR = membrane signal switch"

Avoid:

- "The hydrophobic core prevents polar molecules from crossing the membrane."
- "This pathway amplifies the signal by activating many downstream molecules."

Put full sentences in the notes, not the slide.

### Prefer non-bullet alternatives

Bullets are allowed, but they should not be the default layout. Prefer a labeled image, comparison, sequence, table, graph, or question when that format teaches better.

When bullets are useful, keep them short:

- 0 to 3 bullets for most slides
- 3 to 5 bullets only when the list is the point
- 3 to 7 words per bullet when possible
- one level of indentation when possible

Avoid nested bullet lists unless the hierarchy is essential.

### Use labels instead of explanations

On diagrams, label the important parts directly. A labeled image usually teaches better than an image plus a paragraph.

### Write useful slide titles

Content-slide titles should tell students what job the slide is doing. Use a short noun phrase, a guiding question, or a brief takeaway.

Good title types:

- topic title: `Gel Electrophoresis`
- question title: `Which band moved farthest?`
- contrast title: `Kinetics vs. Thermodynamics`
- takeaway title: `Smaller DNA fragments move farther`

Avoid long sentence titles. Do not use a clever title if students need a clear one.

## Visual Rules

### Use visuals with purpose

Several pictures on one slide can help maintain attention, especially when the images show contrast, sequence, or examples.

Use multiple visuals when they:

- compare ideas
- show steps in a process
- show examples and non-examples
- connect abstract ideas to familiar objects

Use **2 to 4 visuals** as the normal maximum. More than 4 visuals often becomes noise unless the slide is intentionally a time series, flip book, gallery, or set of alternate views of the same content.

Do not add extra images only for decoration. Each image should help teach.

### Make the visual hierarchy obvious

Students should know where to look first.

Use size, placement, labels, or spacing to show:

- the main object
- the key comparison
- the direction of a process
- the answer to a question

### Use flip books and alternate views

Flip books are encouraged when a concept benefits from motion, sequence, or progressive focus. Use a series of minimal slides to show one small change at a time.

Flip books are preferred over GIFs or automatic animation because the instructor controls the pace. Manual advance lets the instructor pause, ask a question, repeat a step, or slow down when the room needs more time.

Good uses include:

- same structure, new label
- same graph, new interpretation
- same pathway, next step highlighted
- same figure, alternate view
- time series or ordered sequence

Keep each slide in the sequence visually stable. Students should notice what changed without re-reading the whole slide.

### Use diagrams for mechanisms

For pathways, cycles, structures, or processes, use diagrams instead of text-heavy explanations.

Good mechanism slides show:

- starting point
- direction of change
- key intermediate steps
- final outcome

If a process moves through space or time, label the direction. Use arrows and a short phrase, not a paragraph.

### Use examples and counter-examples

Examples and counter-examples make the style guide easier to apply.

Preferred layout sketches:

```text
GOOD: image-led comparison

[ large image A ]        [ large image B ]
short label              short label

one-line takeaway
```

```text
GOOD: guided interpretation

What changed?

[ large graph or structure ]

30 sec alone -> 1 min with partner
```

Avoid these layouts:

```text
BAD: text dump

Title
- long sentence that explains the whole figure
- another long sentence with several clauses
- third long sentence students read instead of listening
[ small figure ]
```

```text
BAD: visual noise

[ tiny image ][ tiny image ][ tiny image ]
[ tiny image ][ tiny image ][ tiny image ]
no clear order or hierarchy
```

For slide layouts, prefer:

- one large image plus 3 labels
- two images side by side with a short comparison label
- a process diagram with arrows and short step names
- a question slide followed by an answer slide
- a before/after slide showing the same concept cleaned up

Avoid:

- six tiny images with no hierarchy
- a figure plus a paragraph explaining the figure
- a full textbook table copied onto a slide
- a screenshot that students cannot read
- a bullet list when a visual comparison would work better

## Quantitative and Procedural Reasoning

### Build worked examples as sequences

Do not compress a worked example into one dense slide. Use a sequence of slides with the same visual anchor, then change only one meaningful step at a time.

Good uses include:

- calculations
- genetics crosses
- table interpretation
- enzyme kinetics
- equation derivations
- mechanism steps

For large problems, show the full problem once, then reduce it to a repeatable method. Keep the method visible while students work.

### Scaffold calculations one step at a time

For intimidating quantitative problems, use this pattern:

1. show the full problem
2. name the method
3. build a table or visual workspace
4. fill in one cell or step
5. ask students to try the next step
6. reveal the answer
7. repeat until the pattern is clear

This keeps students focused on reasoning instead of copying a finished solution.

### Build equations, then name them

For equations, reveal the need first, then the assumptions, then the pieces, then the final named equation. Avoid dropping a finished equation onto the slide before students know why it exists.

Good sequence:

1. what are we trying to measure?
2. what stays constant?
3. what changes?
4. what relationship follows?
5. what is the named equation?

### Use a standard order for lab methods

For lab methods, teach in this order:

1. purpose
2. principle
3. apparatus
4. readout
5. interpretation

This prevents students from memorizing equipment before they know what the method is trying to accomplish.

### Use a standard order for real experiments

For classic or real experiments, use this order:

1. question
2. technique
3. result
4. conclusion

Add a think-pair-share prompt before showing the conclusion when the result is interpretable from the visual.

### Ask what changes and what stays constant

For graphs, tables, equations, and comparisons, use the prompt: "What changes, and what stays constant?" This helps students identify the controlled variables and the meaningful pattern.

Useful prompts:

- What do you notice?
- Which values matter?
- What should we ignore?
- What does this pattern mean?
- What changed between these two views?
- What stayed constant?

## Graphs, Tables, and Equations

### Graphs

Graphs are teaching opportunities, not decorations. Use graphs for graphical literacy and think-pair-share prompts.

Good graph prompts include:

- What do you notice?
- Which values matter?
- What should we ignore?
- What does this pattern mean?
- What is the main trend?
- What comparison matters most?
- What changed between these conditions?
- What stayed constant?
- What evidence supports the conclusion?
- What would you predict next?

Reveal graph interpretation stepwise when possible: first axes, then trend, then comparison, then conclusion.

### Data tables

Avoid full dense tables unless students truly need the table. Most tables should become one of the following:

- a chart
- a simplified table with only the relevant rows and columns
- a zoomed-in crop of the important cells
- a comparison slide showing the key values
- a handout or notes item, not a slide

Use a real table on a slide only when table reading is the learning goal.

### Equations

Use equations when the equation is the object of teaching. Do not use equations as decoration. For derivations, follow the Quantitative and Procedural Reasoning rule: build the equation, then name it.

For equation slides:

- show one equation at a time
- define symbols briefly
- use large type
- connect the equation to a graph, structure, or physical meaning
- put derivations and longer explanations in notes

When students need to interpret an equation, ask what changes when one variable increases, decreases, or stays constant.

## Question Slides

### Use question slides often

Question slides are useful for pacing and student engagement. They also create a pause before a difficult explanation.

Use questions to:

- activate prior knowledge
- introduce a new concept
- create curiosity
- check understanding
- slow down before a difficult explanation

Good question slides are short and direct.

Examples:

- What does this structure do?
- Why does this matter?
- What changes first?
- Which option makes the most sense?
- How would you interpret this graph?

### Format question slides consistently

Question slides should be visually recognizable.

Recommended format:

- centered question
- large type
- generous white space
- one optional image
- no answer on the same slide
- distinct but consistent background or layout

For active-learning question slides, add timing and interaction instructions.

## Media Rules

### Movies and GIFs

Movies and animated GIFs are discouraged but not forbidden.

Use video only when motion is central to the concept. Do not use video as decoration. Prefer manual flip books when instructor pacing matters.

### Audio

External audio is not allowed. Movies should be muted.

## Delivery Rules

### Do not read slides

Slides are visual prompts. The instructor should explain the idea in spoken language.

### Show enthusiasm

Make the topic feel worth learning. Energy, curiosity, and humor help students stay engaged.

### Keep explanations focused

Say what students need to understand the slide. Avoid adding too many side details unless the side detail improves understanding.

### Use your own words

Speaker notes are allowed, but notes should be written in the instructor's own voice. Reading from notes is acceptable when needed, but the delivery should still sound natural.

## Slide Checklist

Use this as a pre-flight gate before class:

- Does the slide teach one main idea?
- Is there a clear visual anchor?
- Is the text short and readable?
- Are complete sentences avoided?
- Are technical terms defined briefly?
- Is the full explanation in the notes?
- Is the slide accessible from the back of the room?
- Is color supported by labels, shape, or position?
- Would a non-bullet layout teach this better?
- Does this slide invite attention, thought, or participation?
- Will the instructor talk through the slide rather than read it?
- Does this slide avoid reading like a handout?

## Practical Defaults

Use this as the starting recipe when creating new slides:

- image-first layout
- short title or question
- 0 to 3 bullets for most slides
- large readable text
- OpenDyslexic for short slide text
- PT Sans Narrow for long URLs
- direct labels on figures
- speaker notes for full explanation
- one main idea per slide
- 2 to 4 visuals maximum unless intentionally a gallery, time series, or flip book
- active-learning beat every 10 to 15 minutes
- ask-before-telling before key definitions and interpretations
- misconception checkpoints for common wrong ideas
- quick minimal slides when advancing a visual story
- flip books for sequences and alternate views
- section dividers for major transitions
- objectives at the start
- summary slide at the end
- lab methods ordered as purpose, principle, apparatus, readout, interpretation
- takeaway slide after each major section
- consistent visual grammar across a lecture series

## Final Rule

If the slide asks students to read too much, decode tiny text, or hunt for the main idea, the slide is getting in the way.

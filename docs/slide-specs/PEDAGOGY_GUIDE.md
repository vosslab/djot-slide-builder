# Slide pedagogy guide

This is the authoritative working specification for teaching with slides. Your current
instructions and recorded preferences in [../HUMAN_GUIDANCE.md](../HUMAN_GUIDANCE.md) take
precedence. Use [COLOR_GUIDE.md](COLOR_GUIDE.md) for color and [VOICE_GUIDE.md](VOICE_GUIDE.md) for wording.
The [AUTHORING_GUIDE.md](AUTHORING_GUIDE.md#authority-and-evidence) explains how
to distinguish explicit preferences, established conventions, and observed habits.

## Practical summary

Make students think, look, and listen. Give each slide a clear teaching purpose and a reason to
follow the preceding slide. Preserve recognizable examples, visual explanations, questions,
methods, and references that students can use afterward.

- Use a visual anchor: a figure, comparison, table, graph, cross, structure, or worked example.
- Ask before telling when prediction or interpretation gives students a reason to listen.
- Keep a useful example recognizable through its question, explanation, rule, and application.
- Introduce unfamiliar terms and notation before students need them.
- Use instructor-controlled steps and reveals when they help students follow the reasoning.
- Keep complete problems and useful study explanations visible in the distributed slides.
- Give each content deck objectives, a summary, and a separate THE END.

The deck structure, preservation rules, early definitions, and repeated references below reflect
recorded instructor preferences. Other teaching patterns consolidate recommendations from the
earlier slide guides; they are methods to choose when useful, not instructions to retrofit every
slide. Research and additional ideas are identified separately at the end.

## Respect the authoring task

### Preserve a conversion

Conversion preserves the existing teaching content and sequence. Keep existing content slides
one-to-one with their sources; splitting a slide requires human approval. Layout, color, and
typography improvements do not authorize replacing examples, adding a different lesson, or
rewriting an explanation. Preserve approximately 98% of original prose, with minor grammar and
clarity corrections; do not turn that approximate target into an exact test.

Missing objectives, a closing summary, and optional early definitions of terms already used in
the lecture are authorized source-grounded additions. Section dividers may identify existing
boundaries without reorganizing content. Other substantive changes follow the current request.
For an authorized annual-source merge, follow
[../TALKING_POINT_DECK_RECIPE.md](../TALKING_POINT_DECK_RECIPE.md).

Preserve source tables, graphs, explanations, hidden states, masks, and answer reveals. Use
`@replaceme` when supported layouts cannot preserve important content or relationships. A
placeholder is preferable to silently dropping material or substituting an unrelated figure.
Clear it only after comparison confirms complete content and sound rendering. See
[../SLIDE_CONVERSION_CHECKLIST.md](../SLIDE_CONVERSION_CHECKLIST.md).

### Develop new material

Within an authorized revision, choose an arrangement that helps students follow the particular
idea. One main purpose per slide is a useful design question, not an automatic command to split
an existing slide. Ask what students should learn, where they should look first, and what the
instructor will add aloud.

Ground new scientific claims, examples, figures, and citations in the permitted source material
and appropriate verification. Do not invent evidence or put an unsupported statement in the
instructor's voice. For figures, reuse the source or reconstruct supported relationships under
[SVG_DIAGRAMS.md](SVG_DIAGRAMS.md); use a labeled placeholder when facts or geometry cannot
be supported. Diagram reconstruction is not categorically forbidden.

## Give each deck a structure

### Orient and close the deck

Each content deck in a lecture sequence uses:

1. Title slide identifying the deck and its teaching date.
2. Learning objectives.
3. Optional definitions of essential terms new to the lecture sequence.
4. An opening section heading, then teaching content.
5. A Summary section with selected reference slides when useful and final takeaways.
6. A separate THE END slide.

Without a definitions slide, the opening section heading is slide 3. Keep the summary immediately
before THE END, with reference figures and worked examples before the final takeaway summary.
The summary teaches the takeaways; THE END signals closure. Announcements follow their own
[ANNOUNCEMENT_DECK_GUIDE.md](ANNOUNCEMENT_DECK_GUIDE.md).

Cover metadata, font roles, and selectable frames are in the
[AUTHORING_GUIDE.md](AUTHORING_GUIDE.md). Keep lectures distributed across their
existing small decks and chapter files; do not merge them merely for tidiness. This also keeps
authoring practical when presentation archives are expensive to save.

### Write useful objectives

Put objectives on slide 2 of each content deck, not only the first deck of a multi-deck lecture.
Preserve existing objectives when present. Write specific, student-centered objectives from the
deck's existing content. Useful verbs include identify, describe, compare, explain, trace,
predict, classify, and interpret. "Understand" needs a statement of what the student can do.

Add a short unit map after the opening orientation when it helps students place the deck within
a larger topic. A chapter map or Part 1/Part 2 relationship can be enough; avoid another detailed
outline that delays the lesson.

### Introduce vocabulary early

Check prior decks in teaching order. A term is established when it was explained, not merely
mentioned. Define essential new terms in the first few slides, before students need them to
interpret a figure or answer a question. Include unfamiliar notation such as `P(...)` and event
labels. [VOICE_GUIDE.md](VOICE_GUIDE.md) owns wording and scientific notation.

Use familiar words and a useful example or visual. For example, **mutually exclusive** means
**cannot both occur**. Keep definitions visible; put delivery-only elaboration in notes and
needed study explanation on reference slides. Briefly recall earlier meanings when useful and
explain a new meaning of a familiar term.

## Make a connected explanation

### Keep the motivating example alive

A short case, puzzle, clinical example, news story, or consequence can motivate a tool. A useful
sequence is case, prediction, scientific method, then return to the case. Preserve the complete
question and its assumptions. Explain the plan before leaving a difficult opening problem and
return explicitly to answer it.

Build from familiar to unfamiliar: an everyday example, a simple question, a definition, a visual
model, mechanistic detail, then an application or comparison. These are possible teaching steps,
not a required six-slide template. Keep an analogy short and leave it behind when the biological
explanation is ready.

Show why a rule is needed before adding another formula. Preserve the same example through the
question, tempting solution, correction, and rule when it supports that connection. Introduce a
new context after the current one reaches a clear conclusion.

In the Lecture 05D example, the opening pollen problem motivates the probability tools. The
four-allele cross then exposes the overlap mistake before returning to the opening problem.
The recorded guidance protects those connections and the original visual explanations; it does
not make every detail of the revised deck a template. See
[lect05d-v2-probability_concepts.djot](../../genetics/LECT05/djot/lect05d-v2-probability_concepts.djot).

### Use dividers at meaningful boundaries

Sections and subsections help students reset and find their place. Keep them visually distinct
and simple: a short title, an optional meaningful visual or preview question, and little
explanatory text. Use the course's established major/minor hierarchy.

The instructor suggested roughly one divider per ten slides for long Genetics decks, placed at
conceptual transitions. Preserve that approximate context; it is not a universal interval.
Headings cannot repair a missing explanation between slides. Put spoken transitions in notes and
enough of the connection visibly for students studying later.

### Compare the meaningful difference

Useful comparisons include before/after, active/inactive, normal/altered, structure/function,
similar terms, and mechanisms students confuse. Make the relevant relationship visible. Ask:
"How are these similar, and what is the one difference that matters?"

Use consistent layouts for recurring jobs: objectives, definitions, questions, mechanisms,
comparisons, section dividers, and summaries. Students can then follow the content without
relearning the arrangement.

## Teach through visuals

### Choose a visual that does work

Images are central to the instructor's visual teaching style. Use figures, graphs, structures,
crosses, tables, comparisons, and diagrams to introduce concepts, show processes, interpret
evidence, or connect an abstract idea to experience. Several pictures can be useful when they
show contrast, sequence, examples, or alternate views.

Make the main object, key comparison, and direction obvious through size, placement, labels,
arrows, and spacing. Label figure parts directly when useful. Keep figures large enough to read
and preserve their proportions, including artwork embedded within SVGs.

The earlier guide's suggestion of two to four visuals is a planning aid, not a maximum enforced
on every slide. Galleries, time series, and alternate views can justify more. A clear purpose
also justifies a text-only question, roadmap, definition, announcement, or study reference.
Choose images for their instructional contribution rather than filling an image quota.

Inherited layout sketches remain useful examples:

```text
Image-led comparison

[ large image A ]        [ large image B ]
short label              short label

one-line takeaway
```

```text
Guided interpretation

What Changed?

[ large graph or structure ]

30 sec alone -> 1 min with partner
```

A figure plus a paragraph can be appropriate when students need the explanation. Review whether
the paragraph crowds the figure or competes with listening. Six tiny unrelated images, a barely
visible figure beneath dense text, or screenshots with unreadable labels are warning signs,
not reasons to delete source content automatically.

### Show mechanisms and alternate views

For a mechanism, make the starting point, direction, intermediate steps, and outcome clear.
Use arrows and labels to show what changes through space or time. Preserve meaningful spatial
relationships rather than replacing a diagram with disconnected bullets.

Flip books let the instructor control pace: pause, ask a question, return to a step, or slow
down. Useful changes include the same structure with a new label, a graph with a new
interpretation, the next pathway step, or an alternate view of the same object. Keep the visual
anchor stable so students notice the change without rereading the whole slide.

New sequences can use this pattern within the authorized task. Conversion must preserve source
reveals and follows the existing approval rule for any additional slide splits.

## Build quantitative reasoning

### Scaffold a worked example

Retain the full problem and then use a recognizable workspace. The earlier methods guide's
pattern is useful for crosses, calculations, enzyme kinetics, tables, derivations, and mechanisms:

1. Show the full problem.
2. Name the method when students are ready for it.
3. Build the table or visual workspace.
4. Fill one cell or step.
5. Ask students to attempt the next step.
6. Reveal and explain the answer.
7. Continue until the pattern is clear.

Keep the method available while students work. Use matching colored step references and repeat
the shared roadmap at new problems and useful transitions, following [COLOR_GUIDE.md](COLOR_GUIDE.md).
The shared testcross procedure illustrates this convention:
[three_point_testcross_steps.djot](../../genetics/evergreen/three_point_testcross_steps.djot).

Hints are legitimate teaching scaffolds. Before an independent response, avoid revealing the
part students are meant to work out. During instruction, a hint or partial setup can intentionally
support the next step. Put optional delivery hints in notes or show them later as appropriate.
Assessment advice to avoid cues does not forbid teaching a method visibly.

### Keep tables and graphs recognizable

Preserve tables that organize a comparison, a calculation, or a repeated workspace; table reading
need not be the sole objective. The instructor explicitly values tables. Right-align numeric
values, center table headings, use comfortable padding and middle vertical alignment, and
distinguish total rows. The syntax reference owns the supported table properties.

Keep column widths stable across related question, answer, and explanation slides. Use the same
`table-group` for related tables so the compiler sizes the sequence together. Preserve cell
locations, masks, classifications, and known reference rows through the reveal.

When a dense table is difficult to read, consider highlighting relevant rows, a zoomed crop,
a comparison view, a chart, or a fuller study reference within the authorized task. These are
alternatives to consider, not permission to remove source rows or split an existing slide.

For a graph, first help students read axes and units, then the trend, relevant comparison, and
conclusion. Useful inherited prompts include:

- What do you notice?
- Which values matter, and what should we ignore?
- What changes, and what stays constant?
- What is the main trend?
- What evidence supports the conclusion?
- What would you predict next?

### Make equations usable

Show why the relationship is needed, its assumptions, its pieces, and then the named equation.
For example: what are we measuring, what stays constant, what changes, and what relationship
follows? Keep symbols readable and defined, and connect the equation to a graph, structure,
physical meaning, or calculation.

One equation at a time is often useful, but paired formulas or a complete rule reference may
need to stay together. Notes can hold delivery cues; derivations or explanations students need
later also belong visibly in study material. Ask what happens when one variable changes.

### Teach methods and experiments

The earlier methods guide offers two useful sequences:

| Material | Suggested order |
| --- | --- |
| Lab method | Purpose, principle, apparatus, readout, interpretation. |
| Real experiment | Question, technique, result, conclusion. |

Students need to know what the method measures before memorizing its apparatus. When a result
can be interpreted from its figure, let students predict the conclusion before revealing it.
These are inherited teaching patterns, not authority to reorder a conversion.

## Ask questions with a purpose

### Choose the question type

Use questions to activate prior knowledge, create curiosity, check a concept, or slow down before
a difficult explanation. Choose the task students should perform, not just a question-shaped
title.

| Type | Purpose | Useful treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask before telling | Motivate a new idea | Large question, pause, explanation or reveal. |
| Vocabulary recall | Retrieve a needed term | Question, pause, visual answer, connection to the next idea. |
| Multiple choice | Self-assessment or misconception check | Complete stem, parallel choices, answer revealed later. |
| Think-pair-share | Explain reasoning and compare interpretations | Prompt, individual response, partner discussion, share-out. |
| Graphical interpretation | Read evidence | Large figure plus a specific interpretation task. |
| Misconception checkpoint | Inspect a tempting wrong model | Question, prediction, corrected visual, takeaway. |

For quick vocabulary retrieval, the inherited example is "Name the two types of nucleobases,"
followed by a pause, a visual answer, and a link to the next concept. This is different from an
extended discussion. Mnemonics are optional: default to none unless a short, visual mnemonic is
easier than the categories, names, structures, or order students actually need.

### Make the task recognizable

A short recall question can be large and centered with generous open space. A substantial
problem needs its complete setup, data, assumptions, and task. Preserve the recognizable
problem-statement treatment formerly supplied by textbook screenshots while using editable text.
The exact new problem-panel design remains unsettled; this specification does not invent a
layout or font override. Use existing supported layouts, including question-and-answer for MC.

For a participation task, useful cues are **Predict**, **Discuss**, **Compare**, **Label**,
**Explain**, or **Interpret**. A timing example is "30 sec alone -> 1 min with partner -> share
out." Put expected answers and delivery timing in notes; display timing when it helps students
participate. These examples do not prescribe an interaction at every slide.

Use think-pair-share for important or confusing ideas: first students answer alone, then compare
with a partner, then a few report out. On Zoom, choose a workable response channel or breakout
instruction. MC, open questions, figure interpretation, and discussion can all serve the lesson.
The earlier guide favors pair discussion over MC for in-person feedback without polling; retain
that as contextual advice rather than a universal ranking of formats or a rule against Zoom MC.

### Use mistakes and feedback

Build a misconception checkpoint around a tempting interpretation, let students respond, then
show the correction and state the important difference. Keep it low-stakes. The point is to
make reasoning visible rather than embarrass a student.

For newly authored MC practice, error-derived alternatives can reveal a wrong denominator,
reversed direction, omitted class, or confused concept. A worked-calculation choice can let
students judge the setup without spending time on arithmetic. Seriously absurd choices remain
welcome course voice; retain enough meaningful alternatives for the intended reasoning.

Give students the data and one clear task. Let difficulty come from the biological relationships
and reasoning rather than ambiguous wording. A definition or a brief recall slide can support
a larger reasoning goal; every individual slide need not be an application-level test item.

Verify new answers independently of the explanation that produced them: recompute a quantitative
answer, check it against the source data, or use an independent solver. If preserving a source
exposes a suspected scientific error, record it for a decision instead of silently making a
substantive correction. Existing scoped correction approvals still apply.

## Support study after class

### Keep explanations visible

Students may receive exported slides without speaker notes. Keep full problems and needed study
explanations in the slides themselves. A concise live teaching slide can be followed by a fuller
study reference; do not expand every visual beat into prose.

For material intended only for later study, use `=== layout: reference` with a title and `@body`.
The layout supplies an unfilled light gray outline and the footer "For reference - not covered
in class." Do not repeat that footer in the body. Complete sentences and coherent paragraphs
are appropriate, with readable type and no fixed word quota.

Place study references near their topic or in a review block before the final takeaways. Explain
assumptions, notation, and worked reasoning sufficiently for independent use. The instructor can
point out these slides and skip them during class.

### Repeat useful references

Repeat the few essential rules, diagrams, or procedures in a clearly labeled closing Summary
section. Make each reference self-contained: rule, conditions, notation, and any needed figure
or explanation. Preserve familiar titles, symbols, and colors. Students should be able to find
and use it during homework.

The original Addition Rules and Multiplication Rules slides demonstrate the intended offline
reference function: each gathers the special and general forms. A short takeaway does not
replace that usable reference. Do not duplicate the entire lecture.

The earlier guide suggested three to five takeaway ideas; treat that as a useful planning
default, not a quota. Keep the final summary concise while retaining fuller explanations in
visible references where students need them. End major sections with a takeaway when it helps
closure, rather than inserting one after every arbitrary block of slides.

## Plan delivery and pacing

### Use notes as speaking cards

Use `@notes` for speaking prompts, explanations, transitions, examples, optional jokes, emphasis
reminders, expected answers, and activity timing. Write naturally in the instructor's voice.
Source filenames, slide mappings, repair history, and validation logs belong in separate review
documents, not in the lecture's speaking notes.

Explain the idea rather than routinely reading the slide aloud. Reading notes when needed is
fine; keep the delivery natural. Focus explanation on what students need here. Curiosity,
enthusiasm, and humor can remain part of the teaching voice.

### Use inherited estimates cautiously

The earlier slide guide supplied these estimates, without a separately located direct instructor
statement. Retain them as planning suggestions for fast visual sequences, not facts about the
instructor or requirements for other decks:

- Many minimal visual slides: about 10-20 seconds.
- Overall average in that style: about 30-40 seconds per slide.
- Rough planning anchor: about 100 slides per hour or 150 in 90 minutes.
- A short activity: about 1-2 minutes; fuller think-pair-share: about 3-5 minutes.
- An activity every 10-15 minutes was suggested, rather than an established attention-span law.

Flip books and reveals can increase slide counts without increasing content. A graph, mechanism,
calculation, or interpretation task can need several minutes. Budget the opening, sections,
activities, transitions, and closure against actual teaching time. When reducing a newly authored
lesson, consider optional detail before sacrificing its core reasoning and closure; conversion
does not authorize deleting source slides to meet a time target.

The instructor's 30-40-slide target, allowing up to 50 useful slides, was specific to Lecture 05D
v2. Keep that scope instead of applying either set of estimates to every lecture.

### Choose media for the teaching job

The inherited delivery guidance discourages movies/GIFs without banning them, prefers manual
flip books when pacing matters, and says external audio is not allowed and movies should be
muted. Retain that existing delivery default; no separate instructor confirmation of the audio
restriction was found during this consolidation, so do not describe it as a verified personal
preference. Resolve a conflict with assigned source media using the current task and explicit
instructor guidance, rather than silently changing the rule or stripping source content.

Use motion when it teaches the concept and manual advances when the instructor needs to pause
or return to a step. Preserve course-specific media, including the Biotechnology Veridian
Dynamics ending, under [ANNOUNCEMENT_DECK_GUIDE.md](ANNOUNCEMENT_DECK_GUIDE.md).

## Review the teaching sequence

Use these questions for advisory review, not as a new build or wording gate:

- Is each slide's teaching purpose clear, and does it connect to the surrounding explanation?
- Is there a useful visual anchor, with readable labels and preserved proportions?
- Are new terms and symbols explained before use?
- Can students read the complete question and distinguish it from the answer or scaffold?
- Do tables, figures, masks, colors, and reference objects stay recognizable across steps?
- Do questions invite a meaningful response, with feedback at the appropriate point?
- Are delivery cues in notes and needed study explanations visible in distributed slides?
- Are key references easy to find again near the end?
- Does text density suit the live teaching or later-study purpose?
- Does the conversion preserve content, sequence, voice, and source-specific exceptions?

Use [SLIDE_VISUAL_REVIEW_RUBRIC.md](SLIDE_VISUAL_REVIEW_RUBRIC.md) for rendered classroom
quality. Source checks, rendered review, attended playback, and classroom outcomes are different
evidence. A successful export does not establish them all.

## Supplementary sources and limits

These sources inform possible explanations and choices; they do not override instructor
preferences or authorize redesign:

- [Mayer and Moreno, 2003](https://www.umassmed.edu/globalassets/pediatrics/pediatrics-residency-program/woo-insider/resources/nine-ways-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-multimedia-learning.pdf)
  examines coordinating words and pictures, signaling, nearby labels, and manageable segments.
  It does not establish a universal prohibition on complete sentences or visible study text.
- [Garner and Alley, 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf) compares bundled
  slide-design approaches in an engineering presentation. It supports considering meaningful
  messages and visual evidence, not mandating sentence titles on every slide.
- [Szpunar, Khan, and Schacter, 2013](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1221764110) studies
  questions during online video lectures in two experiments. It motivates considering response
  opportunities; it does not prescribe this course's timing or prove effects in its classrooms.

The BPQ pedagogy guide supplied useful ideas for embedded questions: reasoning from data,
misconception-based alternatives, meaningful difficulty, answer verification, and humor. Bank
generation, randomization, pool sizes, item rubrics, and assessment-specific cue restrictions are
not general slide specifications. The main teaching patterns here come from the repository's
existing slide guidance and recorded instructor preferences.
